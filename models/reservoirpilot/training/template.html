ReservoirPilot

A release policy that only knows what a dam operator knows today. It learned to run reservoirs in a simulator, then ran 244 real US dams it had never seen.

The job

Every day the operator picks a release, in m³/s. Two things can go wrong. If the release falls short of the day's demand (water supply, irrigation, power), that is a shortage. If it rises above the river's safe channel capacity Qmax, that is flooding downstream. WaterData scores a whole decade with one number:

J = mean over days of (shortage / demand)² + 10 × (excess over Qmax / Qmax)²

Lower is better. Demand is the day-of-year median release from the first half of each dam's record; Qmax is that half's 99th-percentile release. The second half (up to 10 years) is scored, driven by the dam's real recorded inflow. The policy sees storage, capacity, today's inflow, the recent inflow, the demand calendar and the first half's inflow climatology. It never sees tomorrow's inflow.

Three reference operators: SOP releases the demand while water lasts and spills when full. The rule curve steers storage toward its seasonal median. Perfect-foresight DP knows the entire future inflow; it is a near lower bound that no real operator can reach.

Where it loses

The ratchet

Each rung had to beat the incumbent on a frozen set of 300 simulated reservoirs by more than 0.01 in SJ = 1 − median J / median JSOP, and stay within 5 % of the incumbent's median J on 15 real dev dams. When the simulator changed (g1 → g4), every rung was re-scored on the new frozen set and every decision re-taken in order.

How it was built

A simulator of reservoirs and rivers

Inflows come from WaterSim, the project's verified hydrology simulator: a generated basin, run through one of four runoff models (HBV, GR4J, SAC-SMA, VIC-lite) and one of two snow models, on real ERA5-Land weather from years outside every exam window or on a stochastic weather generator. Each simulated dam gets a random size (0.005 to 40 years of mean inflow) and a simulated historical operator who runs the first ten years. The task is then built from that operator's record exactly the way WaterData builds it from a real one. No exam dam's inflow was ever used for training.

Calibrating the simulator on dev dams

Learning through the simulator

The reservoir's mass balance and the cost J are simple and differentiable almost everywhere. So the network was trained by running it inside the simulator for up to 1,000 days at a time and back-propagating the cost through every day's storage (an analytic policy gradient). After the policy's window, SOP takes over for another year, so the storage it leaves behind is valued by what it does for the following year. Behaviour cloning of the perfect-foresight DP was tried first: it copies decisions that only make sense if you know the future, and reached only SJ 0.25 on the first frozen set. A classical rule with a flood pool and hedging, tuned on the same simulations, was the bar to beat.

The network

15 inputs → 64 → 64 → 2 (tanh), 5,314 weights. The outputs are two fractions: h, the share of today's demand to release (below 1 means rationing now to save water), and e, an extra release toward 1.25 × (Qmax − demand) to make room before a flood. The dam's mass balance then caps the release by the water stored and spills anything above capacity. Checks: the numpy policy equals B's operating loop to 1e-17 and the training rollout to 5e-7; this page's JavaScript equals Python to 4e-14 (release relative to Qmax).

Limits

    Use it

    The release is numpy-only. reservoirpilot-v1.0.zip holds the policy module, the weights, a demo on real exam dams, the model card and every training script.

    import reservoirpilot as rp
pol  = rp.Pilot.load('weights.npz')
task = rp.make_task(first_half_df, scored_df, capacity_hm3)   # daily storage_hm3, inflow_cms, release_cms
R, S = pol.run(task)                                          # release m3/s, end-of-day storage hm3
print(rp.cost_components(R, S, task))
# or day by day:  pol.reset(task); r = pol.act(t, storage_today)
