Neural network · general relativity · real LIGO data

It never heard a merger. Then it found five.

ChirpNet is a small neural network trained only on simulated black-hole mergers, generated from Einstein’s equations and buried in simulated detector noise. Pointed at real recordings from the two LIGO observatories, it picks out the actual mergers and estimates how heavy they were, in milliseconds. Version 2 adds a check that both detectors agree, full posterior error bars, and a neutron-star model that finds GW170817.

Error bars, done properly

Not one answer. Every answer the data allows.

A second network, built the way DINGO is, reads the same 2 s of strain plus each detector’s noise spectrum and returns the whole posterior: thousands of random draws of chirp mass, mass ratio, spin, distance and inclination that are consistent with the data. There is no sampler and no likelihood loop. One pass through a normalizing flow turns plain random numbers into posterior samples.

Are the error bars honest?

For 400 held-out simulated mergers, where the true answer is known, each line tracks how often the truth falls below a given posterior percentile. If the posteriors are calibrated, every line follows the diagonal. The shaded band is what chance alone allows.

What it learned to be unsure about

  • Chirp mass is sharp, the individual masses are not. The inspiral fixes ℳ; splitting it into m₁ and m₂ needs subtler higher-order phase, so the mass cloud stretches along a curve of constant ℳ. LIGO’s posteriors show the same banana.
  • Distance and inclination trade off. A face-on binary far away and a tilted one nearby look alike to two nearly aligned detectors. The flow found that degeneracy without being told about it.
  • Spin is mostly prior. At these signal strengths χeff is barely measured, and the flow says so with wide intervals, as LIGO’s do.
  • Not shown: its merger-time posterior is wider than it should be (the embedding pools over time), and it assumes aligned spins and no higher modes. Source-frame masses use its own distance and the Planck 2015 cosmology.
Neutron stars · GW170817

Then it learned neutron stars, and heard the most famous one.

Neutron stars are about thirty times lighter than these black holes, so their chirp is slower: it sweeps through LIGO’s band for more than a minute instead of a fraction of a second. A second detector reads 16 s windows with tidal waveforms (IMRPhenomD_NRTidalv2). It was trained only on simulations, then pointed at the real 34 minutes of Hanford and Livingston data around 17 August 2017, from LIGO’s public data release.

The ratchet

Every rung had to beat the last one, or it was thrown away.

Each candidate was scored on the same frozen validation set: tens of thousands of windows of simulated noise and held-out waveforms the network never trained on. Only an improvement moved the ratchet. The real LIGO events were never used to choose anything; they are shown here as the final exam.

The rules changed three times, in the open

Rule 1 kept a rung only if sensitivity in Gaussian noise went up. Rung 3 lost 0.01 of it, well within measurement noise, while learning to ignore glitches, and rule 1 threw it away. That showed the rule was guarding one axis only.

Rule 2 scores one number: Gaussian sensitivity + glitchy sensitivity − 2 × chirp-mass error. Rung 3 re-judged under it was kept, rung 4 beat it, and test-time averaging (rung 5) did not earn its 3× cost.

Rule 3 came from a stress test on time-slid real data: a loud chirp in one detector alone also fools rung 4. Rung 6 trained against that and fixed it, but lost a third of its sensitivity and would have missed GW151226 outright. The ratchet rejected it, so version 1 shipped rung 4 with the weakness documented.

Rule 4 counts only loud one-detector chirps (SNR 12 and up), since a quiet chirp in one detector really is ambiguous. Under it, a fix must halve those false alarms and keep the objective within 0.01. Rung 7 (louder fakes) and rung 8 (a new two-stream network that reads each detector separately, then compares them) both cut the fakes to a few percent but cost too much sensitivity.

Rung 9 stopped asking one network to do both jobs. Rung 4 proposes candidates and rung 8 confirms that both detectors agree, with a veto level chosen on fresh simulations before the ratchet saw it. It kept the sensitivity, became more robust to glitches, cut loud one-detector fakes from 99.6% to 17%, and was kept. That 17% is what is left of the flaw.

How it works

Physics in, physics out.

The chirp

Two black holes spiraling together radiate gravitational waves whose frequency sweeps upward faster and faster. To leading order the sweep depends on one combination of the two masses, the chirp mass:

ℳ = (m₁m₂)3/5 / (m₁+m₂)1/5
f(τ) = π⁻¹ (5/256τ)3/8 (Gℳ/c³)−5/8

The violet curve on the spectrograms is that formula, drawn with the network’s estimate of ℳ and the merger time.

The training data

Waveforms come from IMRPhenomD, a model of the full inspiral, merger and ringdown calibrated to numerical relativity. Each one is projected onto Hanford and Livingston for a random sky position, with the real light-travel delay between the sites, then whitened with LIGO noise spectra and dropped into fresh random noise at a chosen loudness.

The real data

32-second public recordings from the Gravitational Wave Open Science Center, whitened with their own noise spectrum. To measure false alarms on real noise, Livingston is slid against Hanford by whole seconds, so no true signal can line up, and the network scans every shift. Before the network sees anything, any excursion beyond 25σ is gated out, as LIGO’s own searches do.

Use it

One Python file, four weight files, any GWOSC recording.

The release runs on plain numpy. No deep-learning framework needed. Point it at two detectors’ strain and it returns candidate mergers with their chirp mass, mass ratio, component masses, a coincidence check and a false-alarm rate measured on real noise. One more call gives the full posterior.


      

    

    

      
What it can’t do (yet)

      
    
        
  • The black-hole detector was trained on aligned-spin binaries between about 6 and 45 M☉ in chirp mass (detector frame). Precessing spins, higher modes and very heavy systems are outside what it has seen. Neutron stars have their own model, tested on one real event.
    • 
        
  • The detector itself estimates detector-frame masses. The posterior network adds distance and source-frame masses, but it assumes aligned spins, and its merger-time posterior is too wide to use.
    • 
        
  • Its false-alarm rates come from about — hours of time-slid real noise, so its significance numbers are upper limits, not the one-in-millions figures of LIGO’s full pipelines.
    • 
        
  • The coincidence check is not perfect. About 1 in 6 loud one-detector chirps still gets through in simulation, and GW151226’s Hanford signal on its own still scores above threshold. Every hit reports the checker’s score and each detector alone (coincidence, h1_alone, l1_alone).
    • 
        
  • The neutron-star model has no coincidence check yet: Hanford or Livingston alone, with the other detector’s data slid away, still scores above its loudest background. It has seen one real event, and its 16 s windows give up the early inspiral, so its chirp mass is good to about 2% on simulations, versus LIGO’s 0.01%. On GW170817 it came out 5% low, with an error bar too narrow to cover LIGO’s value.
    • 
        
  • All five real events are from 2015–2017 (O1 and O2). Later observing runs have different noise; whitening absorbs most of that, but it is untested there.
