What does each of the collection's simulators actually believe, in one line? LawFinder asks each simulator thousands of questions, searches for the shortest equation that answers them, and then grades that equation once on real data it never saw, beside the textbook law and the collection's network.

The exam, all laws

Is each belief true in the real world?

Each law was chosen on simulations and a small real dev set, then run once on the platform's real exam. The verdict is a win only if the law beats every rival on the row's metric.

How it was built

The recipe, with equations instead of networks

  1. Real physics. Each platform's shared simulator (WaterSim, FireSim, GridSim, BuildingSim, RoadSim), already checked against independent references, used unchanged.
  2. Simulated banks. Thousands of simulated basins, fire-days, grid trips, homes and car trips, built under each platform's split rules (free forcing windows, guarded landscapes, train-input years, the prior split, RoadData's train-noise routes).
  3. Symbolic regression on simulations only. An evolutionary search over expression trees (+ − × ÷ power, exp, log, sqrt), with every constant fitted by least squares. It keeps the best equation at every size: the Pareto front of complexity against error.
  4. The ratchet. The rungs are the front's equations from simplest to most complex, then each newer physics version. A rung is kept only if its R² on a frozen simulated set beats the incumbent by more than 0.01 and its error on the real dev set gets no more than 5 % worse. After a physics change every rung is re-scored and every decision re-taken.
  5. Error bars. The residual spread on the frozen set, widened once (never narrowed) to cover 68 % of the dev residuals.
  6. The exam, once. A separate script per platform, beside the textbook law with its constant fitted on the same dev set, the collection's network, and a dumb baseline.

Why not PySR?

PySR needs Julia (about 1 GB with its packages) and a long compile on the two shared cores. gplearn has no constant fitting, so its laws come out with long chains of random constants. LawFinder uses a 300-line numpy/scipy engine in the PySR style: island populations, regularised evolution, Levenberg–Marquardt constants, and a hall of fame per complexity.

What "complexity" means

The number of nodes in the expression tree. 0.395^φ has 3: a constant, a power and a variable.

Seeds

The water search was seeded with Fu's Budyko curve, so the search could pick the textbook form if it was the best. It did not.

    from lawfinder import LawFinder
lf = LawFinder()
