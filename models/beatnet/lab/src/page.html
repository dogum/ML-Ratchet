BeatNet

A heartbeat classifier that has never seen a labelled heartbeat from a real patient. It learned from a simulated heart: electrical activation spreading through a voxel model of the ventricles and atria, projected onto an ECG lead and buried in real recorded noise. Here it reads real Holter recordings of 22 patients it never met.

Research and teaching model. Not a medical device. Do not use it for diagnosis or treatment.

Real recordings, read live

Each record is one minute of modified lead II from the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database exam set (DS2). The minute was drawn at random, by a fixed seed, among the minutes that contain the record's rarest beat class. Your browser runs the network on every beat. The whole heart's electrical activity at each millisecond adds up to one arrow, the heart dipole. Its tip draws a P loop (small, orange), a QRS loop (large, ink) and a T loop (blue). A lead is a fixed direction on the chest: its voltage is the dipole's length along that direction (dashed lines, Dower lead vectors). MIT-BIH records modified lead II, so BeatNet reads the projection on II. A ventricular focus swings the QRS loop to a new direction and makes it slower and wider; that is all a PVC is, to a lead.

Where it fails

    The ratchet

    Each rung changes one thing. It is kept only if it beats the incumbent by more than 0.01 macro-F1 on a frozen simulated test set AND does not lose more than 0.02 of S/V F1 on six real development records. The exam column is shown for the record; it never decided anything.

    How it was built

    1 · Activation

    A 2 mm voxel heart: thick left ventricle, crescent right ventricle, thin atria. Shortest-path (eikonal) spread: muscle 0.2–0.4 mm/ms, Purkinje endocardium 1.2–2.2 mm/ms, atria 0.75–1.1 mm/ms.

    2 · Dipole

    Every voxel's membrane potential rises when the wave arrives and falls an action-potential duration later. The heart vector is the volume sum of −grad Vm: QRS from the activation-time gradients, T from the repolarisation-time gradients.

    3 · Lead

    Random heart orientation and electrode placement, Dower lead vectors measured on Frank's torso model, breathing modulation. Beat types come from where activation starts: sinus node, ectopic atrial focus, ectopic ventricular focus, or both at once (fusion).

    4 · Real noise

    Baseline wander, electrode motion and muscle noise recorded for the MIT-BIH Noise Stress Test Database, quiet background from dev records, amplifier noise, Holter filters and an 11-bit, 5 µV converter.

    5 · Network

    A 1-D CNN (170 k weights) reads a 2.5 s window, the median of the 40 neighbouring beats and six RR features. Trained on 24,000 to 40,000 simulated beats per rung, never on a real one.

    Physics checks

    Beyond ECGSYN

    ECGSYN (McSharry et al. 2003) draws one lead as five Gaussian bumps on a limit cycle. BeatNet uses ECGSYN's RR model for heart-rate variability and checks its average beat against ECGSYN's, but the shapes come from where activation starts and how fast it spreads. A ventricular focus, a blocked bundle or a fused beat is a change of physics, not a new bump.

    Rhythm

    Sinus RR with Mayer-wave and respiratory variability; atrial fibrillation (irregular RR, 4–9 Hz fibrillatory waves) and flutter (sawtooth, 2:1–4:1); premature ventricular beats keep the sinus clock running, so the pause is fully compensatory; atrial premature beats reset it and arrive at 0.28–0.60 of the RR (measured on the dev records: real S beats came much earlier than the textbook simulator's). Bigeminy, trigeminy, couplets and short runs.
